Before your first sourdough loaf · November 7, 2026

Sourdough starter basics: feeding ratios, gift kits & where to begin

A jar on the counter can look like the beginning of a charming baking story. It also comes with a very ordinary question: what exactly am I supposed to do with it?

Begin with the instructions for your starter

Live starter, dried starter and a from-scratch starter recipe need different starting steps. Identify what you have before borrowing a feeding schedule from another recipe.

What is a sourdough starter?

A starter is a culture of flour and water that supports the microorganisms used to raise sourdough. Maintaining it means refreshing it with flour and water. It is an ongoing ingredient, rather than a single packet you use up and replace.

For a complete from-scratch method, use King Arthur Baking’s sourdough starter recipe. Follow the whole method, including the initial flour choice and later feedings. This page is an orientation guide, not a substitute recipe.

What does a 1:1:1 feeding ratio mean?

It means equal weights of existing starter, flour and water, in that order. For example, 50 g starter plus 50 g flour plus 50 g water makes 150 g total. These are weights, not equal cup measures. It is one feeding ratio—not a promise that every starter will be ready in the same number of hours.

King Arthur’s feeding and maintenance guide gives separate directions for counter and refrigerator storage. Choose the routine that matches the instructions you are following. Our guide to using a kitchen scale explains how to tare a container between additions.

What should I write in a starter notebook?

Keep the notes simple enough that you’ll actually make them. Record the feeding time, the amounts used, the room conditions and what you observe afterward. A photograph taken at the same point in your routine can help you compare days.

Give yourself a clean page for questions. “Was the kitchen colder today?” is a useful observation. “I must be terrible at this” is not a measurement. The notebook is there to make the next decision easier, not to grade your kitchen.

Does a sourdough kit include starter?

Not always. Some packages contain a jar and tools; others include live or dried culture. Read the contents list, then check the supplier’s directions. A jar, scraper and a pretty label cannot tell you how a particular culture should be activated.

If you’re giving the kit as a present, add a card naming the reference you expect the recipient to use. Ask whether they want the project first. A living ingredient is a different gift from a decorative kitchen object.

What equipment is useful before the first loaf?

Check the recipe before buying. A scale helps when the instructions use grams. A clean container lets you keep the culture; a notebook holds your observations. Bread tools such as scrapers serve later mixing and shaping jobs. Avoid treating a long accessory list as an entry requirement.

Which recipe should I bake first?

Pick one complete beginner recipe and read it through before mixing. Check its required starter condition, equipment, timing and cooling steps. Keep that recipe open rather than combining timings from several different methods.

If you want to bake while you’re learning about starters, the evaporated milk sandwich bread in our recipe box uses commercial yeast. It’s a separate kind of bread; there’s no need to make every baking day wait for a sourdough project.
