How to use a kitchen scale for bread—without losing your place

Weighing ingredients should make baking simpler. The trick is to give the tare button one job at a time—and remember that a zero on the screen does not mean the bowl weighs nothing.

Returning to the counter, our imaginary baker faced the ancient question: “Was that the third cup, or the fourth?” A scale would not find the shoe. But with flour weighed to the recipe’s gram amount and that ingredient ticked off on the page, it could spare the dough a guessing game. A small victory. On a busy baking day, small victories count.

The weighing routine

  1. Set the scale on a firm, level counter and choose grams.
  2. Place the empty bowl on the platform. Make sure you can see the display.
  3. Press tare so the display reads zero.
  4. Add the first ingredient slowly as you approach the target.
  5. Mark that ingredient on your recipe, then tare before the next addition.

The bowl still counts toward capacity

Suppose your bowl weighs 900 g and the ingredients together weigh 1,700 g. The scale is carrying 2,600 g, even after you reset the display. Compare the maximum load with the bowl plus the entire batch.

That matters when you double a recipe. Tare hides the starting weight on the screen; it does not remove the physical load.

Is the Escali Primo enough for your bread baking?

A scale has two jobs at the mixing bowl: carry the whole batch and show useful changes as you add ingredients. Those are different questions.

The manufacturer's current Primo page lists a maximum load of 5,000 g (11 lb), tare and automatic shutoff after four minutes of inactivity. That capacity includes the bowl. A heavy mixing bowl does not become weightless when you press zero.

Before buying, set your usual bowl on your current scale and note its empty weight. Add up the ingredient weights for the largest recipe you actually make. The bowl and the batch both need to fit within the new scale's capacity. Check the display position too: an enormous bowl can turn a perfectly readable screen into a game of peekaboo.

For small yeast doses, look beyond a promise of “precise weighing.” A display that changes in whole grams cannot show 2.5 g. More decimal places are not, by themselves, proof of accuracy either. Choose a tool with a stated range and accuracy suitable for that quantity, or follow the recipe's supplied spoon measure.

When the recipe asks for a pinch of yeast

Use a precision scale with an appropriate stated weighing range and accuracy for very small doses, or use the recipe’s spoon measurement when it supplies one. Extra decimal places alone do not establish accuracy.

For salt and yeast, a small separate cup gives you a chance to correct an extra pinch before it disappears into the main bowl.

If the display switches off halfway through

Do not assume you can reconstruct an unrecorded partial addition. A written ingredient weight and a tick beside each finished step help you resume confidently. If interruptions are common, measuring ingredients into separate containers first may be worth the extra washing-up.
